The cup, the salver and the shield

Metal trophies come in three classic forms, and each one means something slightly different. The cup is the winner's trophy — tall, handled, and unmistakable. It is what a championship final deserves, and it is the shape a team lifts. The salver is a flat, formal tray, usually silver, and it suits presentations, long service and ceremonial occasions where a cup would be too much. The shield is the traditional school and club award, warm and understated, and it hangs well on a wall.

Within those forms, the size is tailored to the occasion. A league winner's cup is tall; a runner-up cup is a step down; a category winner's cup sits between them. Getting the sizes right across a set is part of the work — the pieces should look like they belong together, with the hierarchy clear at a glance.

Metal for tournaments and leagues

Tournaments and leagues are where metal trophies earn their keep. A season is long, the final matters, and the trophy has to feel like the occasion. That means the right cup for the winners, matching pieces for runners-up and categories, and medals for the wider field — all engraved consistently, so the set reads as one event rather than a collection of unrelated pieces.

For a tournament, the most useful details to send are the name of the event, the categories, the number of pieces in each category, and the date of the final. Names for engraving can follow later — the cups and the layout can be settled first, and the engraving list added once the winners are known. That is how most tournaments order: design and production first, final names last.

Engraving metal

Metal takes engraving cleanly, and the wording is what turns a cup into a record of a win. The tournament name, the category, the year and the recipient's name are the usual elements, and the layout is set out for your approval before it is cut. A logo can be engraved as well. The engraving is included with every piece.

Because metal trophies are often ordered in sets, consistency matters. The same wording style, the same placement, the same depth of cut across the whole set — that is what makes a tournament's trophies look like they were made together, which they were.
